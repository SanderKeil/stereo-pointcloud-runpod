Piezo Walker Rev C

Four piezo calipers grip a hardened Ø12 rotor and walk it round. Two calipers grip and push while the other two let go and step back, then they swap. Each clamp is held shut by a flexure spring and opened by its own small stack, so the rotor stays locked with the power off. Each push comes from an 18 mm stack standing along the shaft, amplified about 2× by a flexure bell-crank.

Amplification trades force for stroke. It makes the actuator 1.5–2× faster below half torque. At full torque it's slightly slower than a direct push, because a 3×3 mm stack only has about 290 N to trade. To be faster at full torque the push stacks need more cross-section, and there's no room for that in Ø20 with four stations. The other ways to go faster are a higher step rate (small stacks run comfortably at 2–5 kHz, which multiplies these speeds) or a longer package with a second rotor.

One step

  1. A holds the rotor. B's clamp stacks open their jaws.
  2. A's push stacks extend. The bell-cranks swing A's calipers sideways and the rotor turns with them. At the same time B's opened calipers swing back the other way.
  3. B's clamp stacks switch off and the preload leaves close B's jaws on the rotor.
  4. A's clamp stacks open A's jaws. B now repeats the push while A steps back.

A pair always closes before the other pair opens, so the rotor is never free. Each clamp's force closes inside its own caliper: the upper jaw, rotor and lower jaw form one loop, so the bearings carry no clamp load.

Why a bell-crank, not a diamond

A diamond (bridge) amplifier around the stack is the usual choice, but at a useful ratio it is about 10 mm wide. Four of them don't fit in a Ø20 circle. The bell-crank is a flexure lever: the stack lifts a 1.9 mm input arm, and a 4.0 mm output arm turns that into tangential motion of the caliper. It fits beside the stack in about 5.5 mm, and it can be cut into the same wire-EDM stator as the posts and hinges.

Not drawn yet: a parallel-leaf guide under each caliper, so it moves purely sideways, and spherical seats on the stack ends. Both are needed so the stacks only see axial load.

Open risks, in the order to test them

  1. Lost motion. A 10–24 µm step leaves only a few microns for jaw compliance, pad deformation and the end-cap seats. Build one leg and measure the step under 60 N before anything else.
  2. Clamp opening margin. The clamp stack can only just open a 100 N preload (1.5× margin), and it needs to open about 2 µm beyond. Rotor thickness and jaw parallelism must be held to about a micron, or some calipers won't open.
  3. Contact wear. Each pad sees about 10⁸ cycles in 28 hours at 1 kHz. Pad material and rotor finish set the life.
  4. Stiffness. The amplified legs are softer (~3.4 N/µm at the caliper), giving 0.19° of wind-up at full torque. Closing all four calipers when holding stiffens it.
  5. Heat at high step rates. The stacks are small, so heat is modest at 1 kHz. At 3–5 kHz, check stack temperature.
