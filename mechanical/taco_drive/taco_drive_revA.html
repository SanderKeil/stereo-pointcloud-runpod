Taco Drive Rev A

A flat spring-steel disc, clamped on the output hub, is pressed down at two opposite rim points onto a fixed anvil ring. The sheet folds about one axis only, like a taco. A rotating two-roller carrier moves the fold axis around, and the disc creeps forward by the arc length the fold takes up: about 0.34 mm of rim per wave revolution.

How it moves

Between the flat hub clamp and the flat land on the anvil, the sheet takes the elastic S-curve of a beam with both ends held flat:

f(s) = δ (3s²/L² − 2s³/L³) Δx = ∫ ½ f′² ds = 0.6 δ²/L κmax = 6δ/L² → σ = 3 E t δ / L²

The rim material under each roller is pinned to the anvil by friction while the fold axis sweeps past. No slip at the contact gives an output rate of Δx/rc per radian of input, in the same direction as the carrier.

Because Δx goes with δ², the ratio is set by the anvil height relative to the hub face. A shim change of 0.01 mm moves the ratio by about 0.6. One housing can be tuned anywhere from about 110:1 to 180:1 with shims alone.

Why rollers, not discrete clamps

The concept model used 12 plunger clamps firing in pairs. On paper that is fine; in metal it over-constrains the sheet at every hand-off:

  • While pair A grips and pair B lands, both rim points are pinned to the frame, but the fold axis still has to turn 15° to sit between them. The sheet can only follow by slipping at a pad or by stretching. Each hand-off is a 0.09 mm scrub at the pads.
  • That scrub is the whole output motion, so the pads would wear at the drive's own step size, and the output would tick rather than glide.

A roller is a clamp whose contact point moves continuously. The material under it is momentarily fixed to the anvil, the fold axis turns smoothly, and the output glides. It also replaces 12 plungers, 12 springs and a timing cam with two arms. Discrete clamps stay an option if you want power-off holding; they would need tangentially compliant pads (≈0.1 mm).

Open risks, in the order to test them

  1. Does the sheet really fold as a taco? The model assumes the sheet takes pure single curvature with the land lying flat on the anvil for about ±23° around each roller. Under two point loads it may instead cone or twist near the hub. Test first with a 3D-printed fixture, a 0.15 mm shim-steel disc and a dial gauge, or with a shell FEA run. If the shape is wrong, the ratio and stress are wrong too.
  2. Fatigue. ±331 MPa against an assumed ±500 MPa endurance gives a margin of 1.5. Hub-clamp fretting and edge finish will set the real life. Get fatigue data for the actual foil and finish.
  3. Friction coefficient. Slip torque scales with μ. Dry hardened steel on spring steel is 0.15–0.3 and changes with any oil film. Measure it on the rig; a thin hard coating on the anvil (DLC or thermal-sprayed carbide) is the fallback.
  4. Shear wrinkling at the hub at full torque. Watch for it on the rig. If it appears, use a larger hub clamp, with some loss of bend span.
  5. Roller spin and efficiency. A 5 mm wide roller on a 47.5 mm track scrubs about 10% across its face. The crown keeps the contact narrow, and a conical roller would remove the scrub entirely. Measure input vs output torque.
  6. Patent check. A roller or cam pressing a flexible disc against a ring is close to Genesis's mechanical wave-generator variants. The single-curvature fold and the flat land are the distinguishing features. Get a professional opinion before you publish or file.
